Mission Espada

Read the southernmost mission through its compact church, working loom, farm-and-ranch history, and the most complete original acequia system in the national park.

Why this place matters

A compact mission whose waterworks reveal an expansive agricultural and technical system.

Mission Espada began in East Texas in 1690 and moved to the San Antonio River in 1731. Its mission-era church, weaving story, farmlands, ranching network, aqueduct, and still-legible acequia system show how water engineering and skilled labor sustained a settlement.

Water across a low creek

The 1745 Espada Aqueduct carried acequia water while preserving the elevation needed to move it toward mission fields.

Craft and material

A working historic loom interprets how mission residents turned ranch wool and field-grown cotton into cloth. Ask whether demonstrations are available.

The landscape was larger

Fields and distant ranches supported crops, cattle, horses, sheep, and goats; the church compound was only one part of the operating mission.

World Heritage context

This is one of five components in the UNESCO San Antonio Missions World Heritage Site. It remains a distinct place with its own operator, access rules, and living history.

Plan a responsible visit

  • Visit the mission first, then use the official park information to locate the separate Espada Aqueduct safely.
  • Park facilities generally operate 9 a.m.–5 p.m.; exterior grounds are open sunrise–sunset.
  • Admission and parking are free. Contact-station staffing and loom demonstrations can vary.
  • The church remains an active parish and may close for worship or special events.
  • Pets are allowed on grounds and trails on a leash no longer than six feet, but not in the church or federal buildings.

Observe without erasing history

Nature and culture are intertwined here. Notice water, stone, plants, animals, heat, and shade while respecting active worship, descendant communities, archaeology, and protected structures.
