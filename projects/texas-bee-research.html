Minimum field record

  • Date, start time, duration, and general location
  • Weather, temperature, wind, and recent rain
  • Plant or nesting surface
  • Behavior: foraging, nesting, mating, resting, hunting
  • Photographs from more than one angle when possible
  • Identification level and confidence
  • A clear record of zero sightings

Questions shaped by Texas.

South Texas observations need to account for heat, irregular rain, long growing seasons, urban irrigation, and the meeting of several ecological regions.

Heat and activity

  • When does midday heat reduce visible foraging?
  • Do irrigated gardens extend activity during drought?
  • Which species appear only in spring or after rain?

Bloom continuity

  • Where are the spring, summer, and fall forage gaps?
  • Which plants provide pollen as well as nectar?
  • Do native and ornamental blooms attract different groups?

Nesting substrate

  • How much bare, undisturbed, well-drained soil remains?
  • Which hollow stems, dead wood, or cavities are occupied?
  • How do mulch and mowing alter nesting opportunity?

Observation bias

  • Are large carpenter and bumble bees masking small sweat and masked bees?
  • Do photographs support species-level identification?
  • Which parks and neighborhoods remain undersampled?

Plants, nesting, and stewardship.

Habitat advice should be regional, pesticide-aware, and based on more than flower color. Preserve nesting structure and season-long resources alongside bloom.

Identification boundary

Many small bees require microscopic characters, a specimen, or an expert determination. A careful family or genus record is better than an unsupported species name.
