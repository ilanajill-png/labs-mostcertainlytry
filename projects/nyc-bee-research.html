Start with the collection.

The American Museum of Natural History holds an unusually large bee collection and strong Nearctic material. Its research holdings are behind the scenes; access follows the museum's current visitor policy and is not guaranteed by ordinary museum admission.

American Museum of Natural History · Invertebrate Zoology

Hymenoptera–Apoidea Collection

At the time this page was researched, AMNH described approximately 475,000 bee specimens, at least 7,100 identified species, 1,174 holotypes, and major Nearctic holdings. The museum also highlights Jerome G. Rozen Jr.'s collection of nests, immature stages, associated adults, and parasites.

AMNH Hymenoptera–Apoidea Collection ↗Research visit instructions ↗
  1. Define a narrow scope. Name borough or region, years, families or genera, and the question the specimens could answer.
  2. Check the current visit policy. Confirm notice, eligibility, hours, identification, handling, and photography requirements before planning around access.
  3. Request labels and records. Collector, date, locality, plant, determination, and catalog number may matter as much as the specimen.
  4. Preserve uncertainty. Record outdated names, ambiguous localities, incomplete labels, and unresolved determinations.

Questions for an urban bee study.

Specimens, garden records, and digital observations represent different sampling systems. Put them beside one another without pretending they are interchangeable.

Historical change

  • Which bees were collected in the five boroughs, Long Island, Westchester, and the lower Hudson Valley before 1950?
  • Which historical localities have changed land use?
  • Where do modern records add species—or reveal sampling gaps?

Seasonal timing

  • How do spring emergence, summer activity, and late-season bees align with bloom?
  • Do dates shift with heat, drought, or an early spring?
  • Which gaps are biological and which reflect observer effort?

Nesting lives

  • Which species use bare soil, stems, cavities, wood, or existing nests?
  • Where do parks, community gardens, green roofs, and vacant lots provide nesting structure?
  • What do nests and immature stages reveal beyond pinned adults?

Plants and provenance

  • Which plants supply pollen, not merely nectar?
  • Which bees show close host-plant relationships?
  • Are plants locally appropriate, pesticide-safe, and available at the needed scale?

Identification boundary

A photograph may support family, genus, or species identification depending on angle, detail, sex, and taxon. Preserve “unknown bee” or a higher-level identification when the evidence does not support more.

Methods and source boundary.

Collection pages, institutional program pages, and first-party organization sites were prioritized. Facebook-only resources are retained as community leads with explicit access and verification warnings.
