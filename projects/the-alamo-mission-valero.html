The Alamo · Mission Valero

Look beyond the 1836 battle and read the Alamo first as Mission San Antonio de Valero: a relocated river settlement shaped by Indigenous life, Spanish colonization, acequia water, farming, craft, and an unfinished church.

Why this place matters

A famous battlefield whose longer mission history explains the origin of the site—and much of early San Antonio.

Mission San Antonio de Valero was founded in 1718 and moved to its present location in 1724. Indigenous residents and Spanish missionaries built a community that used river water and acequias for crops and livestock, practiced farming and skilled trades, and continued Indigenous food and material traditions amid profound colonial change.

Three mission locations

Valero moved twice before reaching the present site in 1724. Water, workable land, building stone, storms, and access to resources shaped those decisions.

An unfinished church

Construction setbacks left the mission church incomplete during the mission era. Decorative painting had begun, and traces remain inside.

Urban layers

The mission became a military post, battlefield, commercial place, monument, and museum. Read the plaza as accumulated history, not a frozen single moment.

World Heritage context

This is one of five components in the UNESCO San Antonio Missions World Heritage Site. It remains a distinct place with its own operator, access rules, and living history.

Plan a responsible visit

  • Reserve a free timed church-entry ticket when available; paid tours and museum experiences are optional.
  • The site is generally open daily 9 a.m.–5:30 p.m.; the Collections Center closes at 5 p.m. and Christmas Day is closed.
  • There is no Alamo parking. Use downtown public parking or transit and check current construction access before leaving.
  • Follow the Rules of Reverence: no flash, open food or drinks inside historic structures, jogging, bicycles, drones, smoking, or professional photography.
  • Pets are not allowed on the historic grounds except service animals; leashed dogs are allowed in Plaza de Valero.

Observe without erasing history

Nature and culture are intertwined here. Notice water, stone, plants, animals, heat, and shade while respecting active worship, descendant communities, archaeology, and protected structures.
