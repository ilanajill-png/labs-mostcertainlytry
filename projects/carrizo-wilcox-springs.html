Texas groundwater field guide

Where the Carrizo–Wilcox reaches daylight

The aquifer has no single river-style headwater. Its springs appear along a broad sandy outcrop belt, from South Texas to the Piney Woods—mostly on private land, with one currently verified visitor destination.

Camp Tonkawa history

A spring, a Scout-era swimming place, and a later Ozarka agreement.

The parcel record and later recollections support several distinct chapters. The Boy Scout period belongs to the 1930s; Ozarka’s documented association is late twentieth century—not the same event.

Before 1912

A natural spring landscape

The Carrizo-fed spring and pond predate modern recreation. The current tourism listing says people have visited the springs for more than a century; no claim is made here about the first human use.

1912

Earliest transaction in the currently indexed parcel chain Deed index

This is the earliest transaction found in the present appraisal and official-record chain, not necessarily the land’s first ownership record.

March 9, 1929

S. B. Tucker acquires the property Deed index

Nacogdoches County’s indexed records place the property in the Tucker family’s chain before the reported Scout-camp period.

1930s

Reported Boy Scout camp and swimming hole Secondary recollection

A 2009 regional-history video describes Tonkawa Springs as a former Boy Scout swimming hole. The exact Scout council, operating dates, and any lease instrument still need confirmation from a council archive or contemporary newspaper.

Late 20th century

Tucker-family trust and Ozarka association

Local accounts place Ozarka activity in the 1980s, but the searchable deed index does not yet prove that start date. The land remained in the Tucker-family chain and later appears under Acrey Mill Pond Trust.

March 1992

Acrey Mill Pond Trust–Ozarka memorandum recorded Official record

The county index records a Memorandum of Agreement between Acrey Mill Pond Trust and Ozarka Water Co. This proves a documented relationship by 1992; examining the instrument itself is still necessary to establish its full terms or whether it memorialized an earlier agreement.

Today

Camp Tonkawa Springs RV Park and campground

The spring-fed pond remains the center of a privately operated swimming and camping destination. Visitors should call before traveling because dependable public hours, day-use fees, and temporary conditions are not posted.

Keep the Ozarka records separateA 1995 TWDB well at Roher Springs in Henderson County is explicitly coded “Bottling” and Carrizo–Wilcox / Wilcox Group. That modern Ozarka source is verified, but it is a different property from Camp Tonkawa. This page does not claim that Camp Tonkawa water was bottled without the underlying agreement or a matching well record.

Camp Tonkawa Springs

The statewide spring inventory codes Tonkawa Springs to the Carrizo Formation. Nacogdoches tourism describes a spring-fed pond where visitors swim, walk in the forest, and spend time outdoors.

Groundwater language

An aquifer does not begin at one headwater.

“Traditional headwaters” works for a river or a culturally recognized spring source. For the Carrizo–Wilcox, the more accurate idea is a long recharge and outcrop belt where rainfall enters permeable sand and groundwater may later emerge as springs or seeps.

01

Recharge

Rain falls on permeable outcrop sands and moves below ground.

02

Groundwater flow

Water moves through connected sand layers, shaped by elevation, pressure, faults, pumping, and nearby streams.

03

Discharge

Where the water table intersects the land surface, groundwater can appear through one opening, many openings, or diffuse seeps.

Indigenous history

Is there a Carrizo equivalent to Blue Hole?

Not in the sources reviewed. No Carrizo–Wilcox spring emerged as a documented counterpart to Blue Hole: a water place explicitly identified by an Indigenous community as sacred and used for ceremony or pilgrimage.

What is documented

Indigenous Cultures Institute names four Coahuiltecan sacred water sites visited during renewed pilgrimage ceremonies: Barton Springs, Spring Lake, Comal Springs, and Blue Hole. All four are associated with the Edwards Aquifer—not the Carrizo–Wilcox.

Read the Indigenous Cultures Institute account →

Southern overlap is not proof

The historic Coahuiltecan region included South Texas and northeastern Mexico, overlapping part of the southern Carrizo–Wilcox belt. That geographic overlap matters, but the reviewed sources do not connect Carrizo Springs to a named sacred-site tradition or ceremony.

A Caddo ceremonial landscape

The East Texas Carrizo–Wilcox belt overlaps the Caddo homeland. Caddo Mounds is a documented sacred ceremonial landscape; TWDB’s regional layers place the site over mapped Carrizo subcrop. It is culturally significant and publicly visitable, but it is not a spring and not a Blue Hole equivalent.

Where: 1649 State Road 21, Alto, Texas

Current visitor information: Tuesday–Sunday, 8:30 a.m.–4:30 p.m.; adult admission $5. The museum is fully accessible, and the Mound Trail plus part of El Camino Real Trail #1 are accessible for most wheelchairs and strollers.

Historic spring landscapes

A long arc across Texas.

The public inventory contains 25 Carrizo, Wilcox, or combined Carrizo–Wilcox entries representing 22 unique site numbers. Some are duplicates, historic measurements, unnamed seeps, or sites whose present flow and access are unknown.

South Texas

Carrizo Springs, Dimmit County, takes its name from local springs once bordered by cane grass. The town is publicly visitable; a presently flowing public spring is not verified.

  • Webb County: one unnamed Carrizo record in the statewide inventory.
  • Dimmit County: historic spring landscape and later artesian irrigation development.

Central Texas

Mapped records appear near Belmont in Guadalupe County, near Soda Springs in Caldwell County, and around Beaver Springs in Milam County.

  • Guadalupe County: two private ranch spring/seep systems.
  • Caldwell County: Soda Springs and one coded spring basin.
  • Milam County: Beaver Springs and a nearby duplicate record.

East Texas

The densest group of named records lies in the Piney Woods and neighboring counties.

  • Nacogdoches: Tonkawa Springs, Bellevue Spring, and one unnamed site.
  • Henderson: Meredith Spring and Roher Springs among several records.
  • Rusk, Harrison, Wood, Bowie: Big Springs, Roseborough Springs, Dumas Spring, and Dalby Springs.

Inventory boundaryThe Springs of Texas dataset explicitly says it does not include every Texas spring. A point can also be historical, duplicated, difficult to relocate, dry today, or located on private land.

Access guide

What can actually be visited?

Public geography and public access are different things. A town, road, or database pin may be public while the spring itself remains private, unmarked, unsafe, or no longer flowing.

Make a nature day of it

Other outdoor places around Nacogdoches.

Approximate one-way driving mileages begin at Camp Tonkawa Springs and are rounded from current road routing. Access details come from the operating agency or destination manager; check alerts and weather before leaving.

About 19 miles · free · dawn to dusk

SFA Gardens

Six themed gardens cover 138 acres with nearly seven miles of trails. The 11-acre Ruby M. Mize Azalea Garden adds loblolly forest, Lanana Creek, camellias, Japanese maples, hydrangeas, and seasonal azalea displays.

Plan a garden visit →

About 22 miles · urban trail · partial damage remains

Lanana Creek Trail

A 6.9- to 7.1-mile corridor through creek forest, the SFA Mast Arboretum, and northern floodplain prairie. The city says the trail has reopened after 2023 flood damage, but some repairs remain; obey caution tape and erosion closures.

Check the city trail notice →

About 30 miles · free · daylight hours

Stephen F. Austin Experimental Forest

Two interpretive loops cross mature bottomland hardwood and pine forest. The 0.8-mile Jack Creek Loop is surfaced and universally accessible; the 2-mile Management Loop is more strenuous. Foot and wheelchair traffic only; leashed pets are allowed.

About 30 miles · reservoir parks

Lake Nacogdoches

A 2,212-acre reservoir with largemouth bass, crappie, sunfish, lotus, and hydrilla. West Park offers restrooms, playground, disc golf, fishing pier, sand beach, and boat access; East Park has restrooms, fishing pier, and boat access.

A sacred Caddo ceremonial landscape with three earthen mounds, a museum, and outdoor trails. It is not a spring, but it adds essential Indigenous context for the East Texas Carrizo–Wilcox landscape.

What to notice

Look for changes between spring-fed water, wet margins, pine–hardwood forest, and drier uplands. Observe from authorized areas and leave plants, animals, rocks, and water in place.

Responsible visitation

Visit the access model—not just the water.

Springs are ecological transition zones and often culturally or historically important places. A careful visit begins with permission, current conditions, and realistic expectations about flow.

Field rules

  • Confirm permission and hours before leaving.
  • Stay on authorized roads, trails, and shorelines.
  • Do not drink or collect spring water.
  • Do not publish private access instructions.
  • Leave wildlife, plants, soils, and artifacts undisturbed.
