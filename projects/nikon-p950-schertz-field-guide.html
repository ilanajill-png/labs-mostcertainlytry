Setting Up For Success

Take one photograph. Nothing fancy.

For my first photo, I ignored RAW, ISO, shutter speed, aperture, and every mysterious menu item on the camera. I left the camera in Auto and took a single shot of a toy Paluxysaurus jonesi ("PAW, LUCK, SEE, saurus"), the Texas State Dinosaur, who was all ready and waiting to assist me with teaching kids how dinosaur trace tracks are made at Crescent Bend Park.

Important note

A memory card IS NOT INCLUDED with the camera. I bought a two-pack of Delkin Devices ADVANTAGE 64 GB cards.

Insert the memory card and battery

With the camera off, open the bottom compartment. Insert the battery in the direction shown beside the slot, then press the SD card in until it clicks. Never force either one.

Charge the included battery

The Nikon P950 comes with one EN-EL20a battery, and a charging cable that connects to the camera (with the battery inserted) for charging.

I've heard that this camera will need multiple batteries because they die quickly and a battery takes three hours to charge. But before I buy any other accessories I want to successfully take, download, and post some photos.

The ON/OFF button flashes green while charging and turns off when complete. My battery came with a little juice in it, but a fully empty battery takes about three hours to charge.

Attach the strap

The P950 weighs a little over two pounds, so I made sure to attach the strap before taking it out for a test run! I also plan on getting a tripod in the near future.

Remove the lens cap

I wish I was joking, but seriously, remember to do this before turning the camera on. I had a mini heart attack that the camera was broken.

Choose Auto

Turned the large mode dial until the Green Camera Icon aligned with the small indicator circle and let the camera make the decisions.

Review your photograph

I wanted to make sure I actually took a photo, so I pressed the Playback Button (the button with an arrow located below the red record button) before I put the camera away.

Shutter buttonThe button under your right index finger. Half press focuses; full press takes the picture.
Zoom controlThe little lever around the shutter button. Move toward T to get closer and W to see wider.
PlaybackThe triangle button that shows photographs already taken.
Multi selectorThe round directional control on the camera back. It moves through pictures and menus.
ViewfinderThe small eyepiece. Use it outside when sunlight makes the rear screen hard to see.

Your pictures on an iPhone

View first. Transfer only the keepers.

The easiest beginner workflow is to review pictures on the camera, then use Nikon SnapBridge to send a few selected JPEGs wirelessly to your iPhone. Nothing is removed from the memory card when you download it.

First: view pictures on the camera

  1. Press the Playback button marked with a triangle.
  2. Press left or right on the round multi selector to move between pictures.
  3. Move the zoom control toward T to magnify a picture and check detail.
  4. Move it toward W to see several thumbnails at once.
  5. Half-press the shutter button to return to taking pictures.

Beginner rule: do not delete pictures in the field. A photograph that looks mediocre on the camera's small screen may look much better on the iPhone.

Do once

Pair the P950 with SnapBridge

  1. On the iPhone, install Nikon's free SnapBridge app.
  2. Turn on Bluetooth on the iPhone. Open SnapBridge and allow Bluetooth, Local Network, and Photos access when asked.
  3. On the P950, press MENU and open the Network menu.
  4. Make sure Airplane mode is Off and Choose connection is set to Smart device.
  5. Select Connect to smart device and press the camera's OK button.
  6. In SnapBridge, tap Connect to camera, choose the P950 or the camera name displayed, and follow the prompts.
  7. Confirm that the same authentication code appears on both screens. Press OK on the camera and tap Pair on the iPhone before the prompt times out.

Start the pairing inside SnapBridge, not in the iPhone's Bluetooth-device list.

After an outing

Download selected pictures

  1. Turn on the camera and open SnapBridge on the iPhone.
  2. In SnapBridge's camera tab, tap Download pictures.
  3. If asked to switch to the camera's Wi-Fi connection, tap OK or Join.
  4. Wait for the memory-card thumbnails, then tap one picture.
  5. Tap Download. Choose 2 megapixels for a quick text or social post; choose Original format for the full-size file.
  6. Open SnapBridge's pictures tab and tap the downloaded image to view it full screen.
  7. Open Apple Photos and check Recents. If it is not there, use SnapBridge's Share button and choose Save Image.

Use Original format sparingly. Full-size files and videos switch to Wi-Fi and take longer. Nikon's automatic Bluetooth transfers are small 2-megapixel copies, and RAW files or movies do not transfer automatically.

The safer full-quality method for a whole card

Later, buy an SD card reader that matches the iPhone connector: USB-C for iPhone 15 or newer, or Lightning for many older iPhones. Turn off the camera before removing its card. Put the card in the reader, connect it to the iPhone, open Photos, tap Import, select the pictures, and tap Import Selected or Import All. When asked, choose Keep on the card. Do not erase or format the card until the photographs exist on the iPhone and one other backup.

“Saved” and “backed up” are different

A SnapBridge copy on the iPhone is convenient. Your memory card remains the original until you copy the full files elsewhere. If iCloud Photos is enabled, wait for syncing to finish—but remember that deleting an iCloud photo deletes it from all synchronized devices. Keep two verified copies before formatting the card in the camera.

What I captured

Ten birds from my first field practice.

All ten photographs were taken at Crescent Bend Nature Park. Most came from the bird-blind feeding area, where the feeders, water feature, and fallen-branch perches brought the birds into view.

About the identifications

Names are based on the visible field marks in these photographs and the location. The two tentative identifications are labeled honestly so they can be revised if better views or additional field notes point elsewhere.

Wildlife and creek rule

If no animal appears, photograph the perch, branch, water, or empty habitat and continue. Do not use flash, call birds, feed wildlife, or approach them. The creek comes first because it is easier and safer to photograph before the light fades.

After your first 50 photographs

Then change a few settings.

Do not do this all at once. Return here after Auto mode and the basic buttons feel ordinary. Change one item, take a photograph, and confirm that the camera still behaves as expected.

Second-stage setup

  • Charge the included Nikon EN-EL20a fully. Add one genuine EN-EL20a spare later if field days require it.
  • Use an SD, SDHC, or SDXC card. Nikon recommends UHS-I U3/V30 or faster when recording 4K video.
  • Attach the strap before carrying the camera; use the hood outdoors.
  • Choose RAW (NRW) + Fine JPEG and the full 16 MP image size.
  • Turn digital zoom off. The 83× optical range is already plenty.
  • Use Normal VR handheld; turn VR off on a locked tripod.
  • Set date, time zone, copyright, and creator information.
  • Pair SnapBridge after the camera is working normally; keep full-resolution originals backed up separately.

Learn these five controls

  • Mode dial: P for wandering, S for motion, A for landscape and detail, M for controlled tripod work.
  • Exposure compensation: a quick way to protect a bright bird, white cloud, or moon.
  • Focus-area selection: place a small area on the animal’s eye; the P950 does not have contemporary bird-eye detection.
  • Snap-back zoom: temporarily widen the view when a subject disappears at long focal lengths.
  • Electronic viewfinder: press it gently to your face and tuck both elbows in to create a three-point brace.
The rule that fixes the most photographs

If the image is blurry, raise the shutter speed before blaming autofocus. At 2000 mm, tiny movements become Texas-sized problems.

Field mission · Hummingbirds

Catch the bird before chasing the wings.

Your goal is one sharp eye, one hovering bird, one behavior sequence, and one short video. Start at moderate zoom, work from a predictable flower, feeder, or perch, and change only the shutter speed when the light demands it.

Start with these five settingsS mode · 1/2000 sec · Auto ISO 100–3200 · Continuous H · Manual (normal) AF area

Nikon's manual includes the original numbered camera diagrams and menu documentation: camera body, ISO sensitivity, Continuous H, and AF area mode.

Set S and 1/2000 second

  1. Turn the large mode dial on top of the camera until S lines up with the small white indicator mark.
  2. Look at the rear screen. Find the shutter-speed number near the bottom; it may show something like 1/125 or 1/500.
  3. Turn the ridged command dial on top of the camera, near your right thumb.
  4. Stop when the screen reads 1/2000.
Finished result: mode S · shutter 1/2000

Let the camera choose ISO from 100 to 3200

Press MENU. You are in the correct place when you see Image quality, Image size, Picture Control, Custom Picture Control, White balance, and Metering.

White balance Metering Continuous ISO sensitivity   ← choose this
  1. Use the bottom edge of the round multi selector to move down the list. After Metering, you will pass Continuous. The next item is ISO sensitivity.
  2. When ISO sensitivity is highlighted, press the center OK button.
  3. A smaller menu opens with two choices: ISO sensitivity and Minimum shutter speed. Leave Minimum shutter speed alone. Highlight the first choice, ISO sensitivity, and press OK.
  4. Choose the single option labeled 100-3200, then press OK. You are not setting 100 and 3200 separately.
  5. Press MENU to return to the shooting screen.
Finished result: ISO-A lets the camera choose between ISO 100 and 3200

Set Continuous H

  1. Press MENU.
  2. Open the camera icon Shooting menu.
  3. Scroll to Continuous and press OK.
  4. Scroll to Continuous H and press OK.
  5. Press MENU to return to shooting.
Finished result: holding the shutter takes up to about 10 pictures at about 7 fps

Set Manual (normal) AF area

  1. Press MENU.
  2. Open the camera icon Shooting menu.
  3. Scroll to AF area mode and press OK.
  4. Scroll to Manual (normal) and press OK.
  5. Press MENU to return to shooting. A movable focus box will appear.
  6. Use the round multi selector's up, down, left, and right edges to place that box over the flower, feeder port, or perch where the bird is expected.
  7. If the arrows change flash or another quick setting instead of moving the box, press the center OK button once, then try the arrows again. Pressing OK switches between moving the focus box and using the quick controls.
Finished result: you—not the camera—choose exactly where it tries to focus

Leave everything else alone for now

Do not add more menu work to your first hummingbird attempt. Leave metering, image quality, autofocus mode, and vibration reduction at their existing settings. Keep the flash lowered and begin at moderate zoom.

Before the bird appearsConfirm S and 1/2000 on the screen. Make one test burst on the empty flower or feeder port. Then wait.

Use the bird's routine

  • Watch first. Find the flower, feeder port, or twig the bird revisits.
  • Place the focus box on that spot before the bird returns.
  • At a feeder, focus a few inches in front of the port—the hovering zone.
  • Half-press as the bird approaches, then make a short three-to-five-frame burst.
  • Leave space around the bird for its bill and wings. Crop later.
  • If focus will not lock at extreme zoom, back up: the minimum focusing distance near 2000 mm is about 16½ feet.
The P950 realityAt full 2000 mm the lens narrows to f/6.5, so maximum zoom costs light and makes a tiny bird harder to locate. Moderate zoom usually produces more keepers.

The 12-capture hummingbird mission

  1. Habitat: one wide view of the flowers, feeder, or garden. P mode · wide.
  2. Waiting place: the empty favorite flower, feeder port, or perch. S · 1/1000.
  3. Perched bird: full body with room around it. S · 1/500.
  4. Portrait: eye, bill, and feather detail. S · 1/1000.
  5. Hovering bird: body sharp with some wing motion. S · 1/2000.
  6. Side view: bird hovering parallel to the camera. S · 1/2000.
  7. Approach: bird entering the flower or feeder zone. S · 1/2000.
  8. Takeoff: one short burst as the bird leaves. S · 1/2500 if the light allows.
  9. Wing-blur experiment: body recognizable, wings translucent. S · 1/250.
  10. Behavior: feeding, guarding, preening, or resting—never provoke it. S · 1/1000.
  11. Bright-sky test: if the bird is dark, try exposure compensation at +0.7 EV, then return it to 0.0.
  12. Video: 10–20 steady seconds at 1080/60p; moderate zoom and no zooming during the clip.

Change only what the photograph needs

  • Bird perched: 1/500–1/1000 sec; Auto ISO 100–1600.
  • Bird hovering: 1/2000 sec; Auto ISO 100–3200.
  • Deep shade: lower to 1/1000 before raising ISO beyond 3200.
  • Intentional wing blur: 1/250 sec.
  • Bird against bright sky: try +0.7 EV, review, and reset to 0.0.

When something goes wrong

  • Picture is dark: lower 1/2000 to 1/1000.
  • Bird is blurry: return to 1/2000 or move into brighter light.
  • Camera grabs foliage: keep Manual (normal) AF area on the expected hover point.
  • You lose the bird: zoom out, find it, then zoom in moderately.
  • Camera pauses after a burst: it is writing to the card; wait.
Feeder and wildlife care

Use one part refined white sugar to four parts water—never red dye, honey, brown sugar, or artificial sweetener. In hot Texas weather, empty and clean the feeder daily or every other day. Do not crowd nests, block access to food, call birds, or keep shooting if your presence changes their behavior.

83× discipline

Use the reach; resist the gimmick.

Long focal length is useful when it improves behavior, safety, or visual meaning—not when it simply makes the number larger.

Find, then magnify

Locate a bird at 300–600 mm and zoom only after it is stable in the frame. Starting at 2000 mm is like searching for a sparrow through a drinking straw.

Read the air

Heat shimmer can erase distant detail even when focus is correct. Long-lens work is sharpest in cool morning air and across shorter distances.

Support the barrel

Cradle the lens with the left hand, pull the strap lightly taut, use the viewfinder, or rest on a railing or monopod.

Burst with intent

Three to five considered frames usually beat fifty nearly identical files. Pause, reframe, and watch for behavior.

Inspect the eye

Feather texture can look persuasive on the rear screen while the eye is soft. Check the eye at full playback magnification.

Crop last

The 16 MP file has useful but finite room. Choose the sharpest expression and cleanest background before cropping.

Practice plan

Four gentle weeks from Auto to field story.

Your first 100 photographs are private practice. There is no posting deadline and no need to understand every setting.

Week 1 · Auto only

  • Take the same 20-picture assignment on three different days.
  • Practice half-pressing before fully pressing the shutter.
  • Use the T/W zoom lever gently.
  • Review pictures, but do not judge or delete them in the field.

Week 2 · Walk outside

  • Keep the camera in Auto.
  • Photograph a still bird, plant, cloud, and building.
  • Use the viewfinder when sun hides the rear screen.
  • Try Bird-watching mode only for a bird sitting still at a distance; Nikon designed it for that situation.

Tell one place

  • Make an establishing view.
  • Add habitat, subject, detail, season, and a closing mood.
  • Record weather, time, behavior, and settings.

Begin a series

  • Return to the same viewpoint.
  • Compare light, water, plants, wildlife, and use.
  • Publish a concise before-and-after field note.

Caption field card

  • What: common name; scientific name only when confident.
  • Where: park or broad area, never a sensitive coordinate.
  • What happened: one observed behavior or condition.
  • How: camera, focal length, shutter speed, and ISO.
  • Why it matters: connect the image to habitat, season, or stewardship.
  • Invitation: ask one answerable question.
Measure usefulness, not applause

Track saves, shares, profile visits, and substantive comments. Likes are pleasant confetti. At month’s end, compare subject, format, focal length, and posting time; repeat the useful patterns rather than cloning the same post.

Edges

Scan where woodland meets grass, water meets bank, or old stone meets vegetation. Ecological edges often concentrate both subjects and stories.

Behavior

Feeding, preening, courtship, migration, thermoregulation, and shelter use carry more meaning than a centered identification photograph.

Change

Repeat a view after rain, drought, flood, cold front, migration pulse, bloom, or moon phase. Photography becomes evidence when time enters the frame.

Field ethics

Reach is permission to stay back.

The camera’s greatest advantage is the ability to make intimate pictures without demanding intimacy from an animal.

  • Never bait, flush, crowd, pursue, or handle wildlife for a photograph.
  • Avoid recorded calls, especially during nesting and migration stress.
  • Never use flash on bats or nocturnal wildlife.
  • Keep exact nests, dens, roosts, owls, and sensitive plants private.
  • Stay on established trails and respect all closures.
  • Ask before publishing recognizable people; take special care with children.
  • Leave fossils, plants, stone, feathers, and artifacts exactly where they are.
  • Do not geotag a residence or private access point.
