Menstrual Blood in Gardens & Medicine

Historical sources imagined menstrual blood as powerful enough to repel crop pests, heal illness, spoil wine, kill plants, protect doorways, or control fertility. The record is real—but it does not support every modern story told about it.

The central correctionAncient sources document ritual pest control and medicinal or magical remedies. They do not establish a widespread historical practice of collecting menstrual blood and pouring it into soil as fertilizer.

Read the evidence carefully

What is actually documented?

The surviving evidence is cross-cultural but uneven. Egyptian and Chinese texts preserve direct remedies; Indian medical traditions theorize menstrual blood as reproductive material; Greco-Roman writers record medicine, magic, and crop ritual. North American evidence is much later and cannot be projected back to Pliny’s time.

Well documented in textsMenstruating women assigned ritual crop-protection roles; menstrual fluid prescribed in topical remedies, amulets, reproductive recipes, and protective magic.
Historically plausible, not measurableSome remedies may preserve women’s medical knowledge, especially where Pliny names female writers and a midwife. Surviving male-authored accounts remain an imperfect filter.
Not establishedA continuous ancient tradition of collecting menstrual fluid, diluting it, and feeding it to garden soil as fertilizer.

Africa · Asia · Europe · North America

The verified record, in chronological order

Dates refer to the surviving manuscript, archaeological context, or modern study—not necessarily the first appearance of a belief. A written recipe records a tradition; it does not prove effectiveness or widespread use.

  1. c. 1550 BCE
    Africa · Egypt

    Papyrus Ebers: first-menstruation blood used on the body

    Remedy Eb 808 directs that a woman’s breasts be bathed in the blood of someone experiencing menstruation for the first time, with the abdomen and thighs rubbed as well, to prevent sagging and excess breast milk.

    Evidence: direct medical text, but roughly 1,600 years earlier than Pliny.

    Read Papyrus Ebers, Eb 808 — Leipzig Academy of Sciences ↗
  2. Before 168 BCE
    Asia · China

    Mawangdui manuscripts: menstrual cloth and blood in remedies

    Medical texts from Mawangdui Tomb 3 record treatments for gu—a category encompassing poisoning, infestation, or demonic illness—including ingesting the burnt remains of a menstrual cloth and applying menstrual blood mixed with cinnamon.

    Evidence: securely excavated Western Han manuscripts buried in 168 BCE, about 245 years before Pliny’s encyclopedia.

    Read the Cambridge history and translated evidence ↗
  3. Western Han
    206 BCE–9 CE
    Asia · China

    Random Jottings on Medical Recipes: a menstrual cloth rinsed into water

    One bamboo-strip recipe says that, for “madness (or seizures?)” accompanied by internal bleeding that would not emerge, a young woman’s menstrual cloth should be rinsed in a cup of water and the liquid drunk.

    Evidence: a direct Han-period text, but acquired through the antiquities market rather than a controlled excavation; its provenance is weaker than Mawangdui’s.

    Read the translation and provenance discussion ↗
  4. Early centuries
    BCE–CE
    Asia · India

    Ayurvedic compendia: reproductive and diagnostic substance

    The layered Caraka Saṃhitā and Suśruta Saṃhitā traditions describe menstrual blood (ārtava or rajas) as a female reproductive material that combines with semen, and assess its color, smell, and consistency when discussing health and fertility.

    Evidence: a near-period medical tradition with later redaction—not a recipe for administering collected menstrual blood and not a gardening practice.

    Read the sourced overview of early Indian medical texts ↗
  5. c. 60–77 CE
    Europe · Rome

    Columella and Pliny: crop ritual, medicine, amulets, and counter-magic

    Columella describes a menstruating woman circling garden beds to repel caterpillars. Pliny catalogs agricultural danger and protection alongside topical remedies, fever and epilepsy treatments, reproductive recipes, amulets, and doorway protection—sometimes credulously and sometimes with explicit disbelief.

    Evidence: direct elite male-authored agricultural and encyclopedic texts. They report claims, not clinical outcomes or women’s own testimony.

    Columella, De Re Rustica 10.357–368 ↗Pliny, Natural History 28.23 ↗
  6. Roman–late antique
    Africa · Greco-Egyptian

    Greek Magical Papyri: menstrual blood in a contraceptive ritual

    PGM XXXVI.320–332 instructs the practitioner to steep bitter-vetch seeds in a menstruating woman’s menses as part of a larger contraceptive procedure involving a frog and an amulet.

    Evidence: a magical handbook preserved from Egypt. It is geographically African and culturally Greco-Egyptian; it should not be generalized to all Egyptian or African practice.

    Read PGM XXXVI.320–332 in Betz’s translation ↗
  7. 1982
    North America · Yurok

    Power and ritual—not an ancient medical recipe

    Thomas Buckley’s cultural reconstruction examines Yurok menstrual observances as sources of women’s spiritual and personal power, challenging earlier interpretations that reduced them to pollution or male exclusion.

    Chronology warning: this is twentieth-century ethnographic reconstruction, not first-century evidence, and it does not document menstrual blood as fertilizer.

    Buckley, “Menstruation and the Power of Yurok Women” ↗
  8. 2001
    North America · Navajo

    Later accounts of danger and disease causation

    Maureen Trudelle Schwarz records Navajo consultants connecting inappropriate contact with menstruating women or menstrual blood to arthritis and rheumatism, with explanations linked to oral histories.

    Chronology warning: this documents contemporary understandings of danger and illness—not medicinal or gardening use, and not evidence from Pliny’s period.

    Schwarz, “Allusions to Ancestral Impropriety” ↗

Comparative finding: the closest securely dated parallel to Pliny is Western Han China. No comparably dated North American record was found, and none of these African, Asian, European, or North American sources documents menstrual blood being used as garden fertilizer.

Field and garden

Ritual pest control—not fertilizer

Pliny the Elder and Columella describe the menstruating body as an agricultural force. Carefully deployed, it could supposedly protect a crop; encountered at the wrong time, it could destroy one.

Crop protection

Walking the field

Pliny reports that a menstruating woman who stripped and walked around a wheat field would cause caterpillars, worms, beetles, and other vermin to fall from the grain. In his Cappadocian account, women moved through fields with garments raised.

Ritual conditions

Bare feet, loose hair, untied clothing

Other versions required bare feet, dishevelled hair, and a loosened girdle. Columella repeats the pest-control operation in verse and prose, embedding it in an elite Roman manual of estate agriculture.

Danger at the wrong moment

The same power could blight

Pliny warns that performing the walk at sunrise could dry the crop. Touch was said to harm young vines and kill rue and ivy; bees supposedly abandoned hives touched by a menstruating woman.

What the text was doing

Managing risk through women’s bodies

The claims joined magic, natural history, and labor management. Recent scholarship reads them as imagined farm-risk strategies that assigned enslaved and dependent women’s bodies a practical role inside the estate.

“If a woman strips herself naked while she is menstruating, and walks round a field of wheat, the caterpillars, worms, beetles, and other vermin, will fall from off the ears of corn.”Pliny the Elder, Natural History 28.23 ↗ · nineteenth-century translation

Remedy, amulet, counter-magic

Medicinal uses documented by Pliny

Pliny introduces the subject as a catalogue of “marvellous powers” and “monstrous effects.” He explicitly rejects the conflicting reproductive claims as monstrous and irreconcilable; elsewhere he records remedies attributed to named authorities, “many writers,” or common agreement. Their inclusion records a medical tradition, not clinical effectiveness.

Agency and attribution

Were women doing this?

Sometimes the record names women as medical authorities. More often it tells landowners what a woman’s body could supposedly accomplish. Those are not the same kind of evidence.

Laïs and Salpe

Female medical authorities

Pliny attributes fever and rabies remedies to Laïs and Salpe. Their work reaches us indirectly, through a male encyclopedist, but the attribution matters: women were not merely patients in this medical world.

Sotira

A midwife’s technique

Sotira is named as the source for rubbing menstrual fluid on a patient’s feet to address recurrent fever and epilepsy. This is the clearest woman-linked therapeutic instruction in the chapter.

Laïs and Elephantis

Contradictory reproductive recipes

Pliny assigns abortive and sterilizing recipes to Laïs and Elephantis, then says their claims about fertility and barrenness conflict and should be rejected.

The missing voices

Prescription is not practice

Pliny and Columella do not give us women’s testimony about the field ritual. The agricultural labor may have been assigned to enslaved or dependent women whose own explanations were never written down.

A modern story checked

Was it an ancient fertilizer?

No dependable source found in this review instructs women to collect menstrual fluid, dilute it, compost it, or feed it to plants. Historical “garden use” should not be silently converted into a fertilizer claim.

What survives

Body-centered ritual

The woman walks, touches, reveals, or circles. The supposed agricultural effect comes from her menstruating presence—not from measuring or applying a nutrient solution.

What does not survive

No demonstrated continuity

Modern “moon blood” gardening may carry personal, spiritual, ecological, or waste-reduction meaning. Without a specific historical source, it should be described as modern practice rather than recovered ancient women’s knowledge.

Later transmission: Roman ideas continued to circulate in medieval and early-modern agricultural and medical books. A surviving early-modern remedy even pairs peony root and castor with a menstrual cloth for epilepsy. Textual survival still does not prove widespread everyday use.

History is not instruction

Modern science and safety

Research on menstrual fluid’s cellular and wound-repair properties is promising, but it takes place in controlled laboratories. It does not validate ancient remedies or home medical use.

Do not treat a historical remedy as medical advice.

Menstrual fluid contains blood, endometrial cells and tissue, immune cells, cellular debris, and other biological material. Human blood can carry bloodborne pathogens. Do not consume it, share it, put it into another person’s food or drink, or apply it to wounds. Avoid using it where other people may encounter it or on edible crops. For health questions, use qualified medical guidance.
