Canyon Lake Gorge

A flood-carved classroom exposing Cretaceous limestone, fossils, dinosaur tracks, faults, fractures, springs, and the workings of the Trinity Aquifer.

Why this place matters

One week of rain opened deep time.

More than 34 inches of rain fell in the upper Guadalupe watershed during one week in 2002. Water overtopped the Canyon Lake spillway and cut a gorge that reveals ancient seafloor limestone and the plumbing of a Hill Country aquifer.

Flood process

The gorge makes erosion visible at landscape scale: water removed soil and rock, reorganizing the channel in days.

Cretaceous archive

Limestone layers, sea urchin fossils, ocean ripples, and dinosaur tracks preserve evidence from roughly 110 million years ago.

Aquifer window

Faults, fractures, seeps, springs, and permeable rock show how the Trinity Aquifer stores and moves water.

Field notes

Read the walls like pages.

Layer

Trace bedding planes and changes in texture or resistance.

Fracture

Notice how cracks steer water, roots, weathering, and erosion.

Fossil

Observe in place; never remove or damage geological evidence.

Seep

Look for where groundwater emerges and supports a different microhabitat.

A calm visit plan

  • Confirm current hours, entry options, and tour availability with GBRA.
  • Wear sturdy closed-toe shoes and bring water and sun protection.
  • Stay on authorized routes and follow guide or docent instructions.
  • Leave fossils, rocks, plants, and artifacts exactly where found.
