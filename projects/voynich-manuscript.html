Beinecke MS 408 · source-led research guide

The Voynich Manuscript

A fifteenth-century parchment codex filled with an unidentified script, botanical forms, zodiac diagrams, bathing figures, cosmological foldouts, and one stubborn fact: nobody has produced a generally accepted translation.

What the object can tell us

History of the manuscript

The book’s materials are medieval, but its language, author, workshop, place of production, and purpose remain unknown. Its illustrations suggest subject areas; they do not provide a translation.

A book with several visual systems

Most pages combine the unknown script with drawings. Modern descriptions group the imagery into herbal or botanical pages, astronomy and astrology, bathing or “balneological” scenes, cosmological foldouts, pharmaceutical vessels and plant parts, and short entries often called recipes. Those labels are descriptive conveniences, not decoded chapter titles.

What scientific testing established

Four parchment samples were radiocarbon dated to 1404–1438 at 95% probability. That dates the animal skins, not automatically the moment every line was written. Yale’s material analysis found inks, pigments, and construction consistent with a historical manuscript; it did not reveal the language or prove any decipherment.

Why “Roger Bacon’s book” does not hold

A seventeenth-century letter reports that Emperor Rudolf II believed the manuscript to be by the thirteenth-century English friar Roger Bacon. The parchment dating makes Bacon’s authorship untenable. The same letter is evidence for the belief and reported purchase—not proof of authorship.

Evidence, not legend

Who owned it?

The early chain is reconstructed from inscriptions and letters. “Reported” means a later document describes the ownership; “documented” means the manuscript, a surviving letter, or a modern transaction directly supports it.

  1. Before 1612

    Emperor Rudolf II, Prague — reported

    Johannes Marcus Marci’s 1665/66 covering letter says the manuscript had belonged to Rudolf II, who reportedly paid 600 ducats and believed it was Roger Bacon’s work. No surviving imperial purchase record has settled the details.

    Reported in a later letter
  2. Early 1600s

    Jacobus Horčický de Tepenec — probable

    A faded ownership inscription on folio 1r links the manuscript to Rudolf’s pharmacist and courtier, also known as Jacobus Sinapius. Imaging made the erased signature legible.

    Physical inscription
  3. By 1637

    Georg Baresch — documented

    The Prague alchemist described the mysterious book in correspondence with Jesuit polymath Athanasius Kircher and sought Kircher’s help interpreting it.

    Surviving correspondence
  4. 1662

    Johannes Marcus Marci — bequest

    Baresch left his library to Marci, a physician and scholar in Prague. Marci later sent the manuscript to Kircher.

    Documented chain
  5. 1665/66

    Athanasius Kircher and the Collegio Romano

    Marci’s letter, still bound with the manuscript, accompanied the gift. The codex entered the Jesuit collection in Rome and later moved with parts of that collection.

    Manuscript + letter
  6. 1912

    Wilfrid M. Voynich

    The antiquarian bookseller bought the manuscript from the Jesuits at Villa Mondragone near Frascati. His publicity campaign gave the codex the name used today.

    Documented modern acquisition
  7. 1930–1960

    Ethel Lilian Voynich

    Wilfrid’s widow inherited the manuscript. After her death, it passed to Anne Nill, a close friend and former assistant.

    Estate history
  8. 1961

    Anne Nill → Hans P. Kraus

    Nill sold the manuscript to New York rare-book dealer Hans P. Kraus. Kraus offered it for sale but did not find a buyer.

    Documented sale
  9. 1969–present

    Beinecke Library, Yale University

    Kraus donated the manuscript to Yale. It is cataloged as Beinecke MS 408 and is now fully digitized for public research.

    Institutional custody

Claims under comparison

Possible translations

“Possible” does not mean “solved.” These are major hypothesis families and published proposals. None is an accepted translation of the whole manuscript.

Open hypothesis

Meaningful language beneath an unknown script or cipher

Information-theoretic and linguistic studies find non-random, topic-sensitive patterns compatible with meaningful text. That supports investigation, but statistical resemblance cannot identify the language or produce a translation.

Proposed · unconfirmed

Hebrew with anagrammed substitution

Bradley Hauer and Greg Kondrak tested an algorithm for unknown scripts and anagrammed substitution ciphers; their model ranked Hebrew highly and produced a speculative reading. The result depends on strong cipher assumptions and does not constitute a generally accepted decipherment.

  • Read the ACL paper ↗
Partial proposal

Proper names and phonetic values

Applied linguist Stephen Bax proposed readings for a small set of plant and constellation labels, using putative names as cribs to infer sound values. The method never yielded a full translation and has not been independently validated.

  • Bax’s Voynich research archive ↗
Published claim · disputed

Proto-Romance shorthand

Gerard Cheshire argued that the text records a previously undocumented Proto-Romance language in an unusual writing system. The proposal attracted substantial criticism over method, linguistic reconstruction, and selective readings; it has not become a scholarly consensus.

  • Read the original claim ↗
Alternative explanation

Generated pseudo-text or elaborate hoax

Gordon Rugg demonstrated that a Cardan-grille-like method could generate Voynich-looking text; Torsten Timm has modeled local copying and mutation as a production process. These studies show mechanisms that deserve testing. They do not prove which process made this manuscript—or that it lacks meaning.

  • Rugg: “An Elegant Hoax?” ↗
  • Timm: generating algorithm ↗
Content inference

Herbal, medical, astrological, or recipe compendium

The pictures make these subject labels plausible and useful for navigation. But an image of a plant does not prove the adjacent words name that plant, and the bathing figures do not prove a medical prescription. This is a genre hypothesis, not a translation.

People and methods

Who is studying it?

This is a selective directory of researchers and research-builders whose work is public and methodologically distinct—not an endorsement of every conclusion and not a complete census of the Voynich community.

Paleography · scribal hands

Lisa Fagin Davis

Used digital paleography to distinguish five scribal hands, sharpening questions about collaboration, production order, and the manuscript’s internal structure.

Read “How Many Glyphs and How Many Scribes?” ↗
Historical linguistics

Claire Bowern & Luke Lindemann

Surveyed the manuscript’s linguistic evidence, transcription problems, word structure, and competing language-versus-cipher hypotheses.

Read “The Linguistics of the Voynich Manuscript” ↗
Information theory

Marcelo Montemurro & Damián Zanette

Measured long-range word distributions and co-occurrence networks, finding organization compatible with meaningful language sequences.

Read the open-access study ↗
Computational decipherment

Bradley Hauer & Greg Kondrak

Tested automated language identification and anagrammed substitution decoding, then applied the method to the Voynich text and proposed Hebrew as a candidate.

Read the ACL paper ↗
Hoax-generation hypothesis

Gordon Rugg

Explored whether period-appropriate tools could generate text with Voynich-like surface properties, offering a falsifiable alternative to a hidden-language solution.

Read “An Elegant Hoax?” ↗
Text-generation models

Torsten Timm

Studies local similarity, repetition, and possible generation algorithms, arguing that copying and modifying nearby tokens can reproduce important textual features.

Read the generating-algorithm paper ↗
Historical research infrastructure

René Zandbergen

Maintains a long-running research site documenting provenance, physical structure, transcriptions, radiocarbon dating, and the history of decipherment attempts.

Explore the Voynich.nu research site ↗
Partial decipherment · historical contribution

Stephen Bax

Proposed a bottom-up method using illustrated labels as possible proper-name cribs. His archive remains useful for evaluating both the promise and limits of partial readings.

Open Bax’s research archive ↗
Curatorial scholarship

Raymond Clemens

As a Yale curator and editor of the 2016 facsimile volume, helped place the object in its manuscript, material, and historical contexts rather than treating it only as a cipher.
